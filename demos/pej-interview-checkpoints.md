# Interactive checkpoints — "Life in the Law: Cynthia Tai, Project Expedite Justice" (ThinkTech Hawaii)

Fifteen checkpoint questions generated from the interview transcript, spaced roughly every 2–3 minutes. Each pauses the video at the moment its content has just been explained, then grades locally with feedback.

The JSON block at the end is drop-in for the platform's interactive video player (`IVQuestion[]`, inline mode): `videoTimestamp` is in seconds, `pauseOnReach` stops playback, `correctOptionIds` grades in the browser.

Note for the SME: this checks comprehension of what the speaker says on camera. A few of her statements simplify the law (e.g. the ICC sentence cap); the feedback flags those so learners get the accurate picture.

## 1 · @ 2:55 — What woke the law up
**The modern body of international criminal law, Cynthia says, really began to develop after which event?**
- A. The Nuremberg trials
- B. **The 1994 Rwandan genocide** ✓
- C. The September 11 attacks
- D. The signing of the Rome Statute
Correct: The tribunal set up after the 1994 Rwandan genocide (the ICTR) is what she credits with developing the body of law — she notes she went to law school before it happened. (Nuremberg and Tokyo were earlier antecedents, but she points to Rwanda as the modern catalyst.)
Incorrect: Listen again around 2:13 — she ties the "big push" to a genocide in 1994.

## 2 · @ 6:00 — What is a "case" at the ICC
**How does a case at the ICC differ from a line prosecutor's case in the United States?**
- A. **In the US a case is one individual; at the ICC you work on a whole country or situation — a militia leader, a head of state, a vice-president** ✓
- B. The ICC only prosecutes financial crime
- C. The ICC assigns three defendants per prosecutor
- D. There is no real difference
Correct: She contrasts "one murder, one victim" work in Kona with being handed an entire country as your case at the ICC.
Incorrect: Around 5:38 she describes going from "disorganized crime" to a militia leader, a head of state, a former vice-president.

## 3 · @ 7:25 — Where the investigation happens
**Why might ICC investigators work from a country in the region rather than at the crime scene itself?**
- A. To save money on travel
- B. **To protect witnesses and let the investigation be done safely** ✓
- C. Because the ICC has no jurisdiction at the scene
- D. To avoid paying local taxes
Correct: It is often not possible to visit the scene safely, so operating in a nearby country keeps witnesses from being exposed.
Incorrect: Re-watch 6:50–7:18 — the reason is safety and witness protection.

## 4 · @ 9:45 — Before you speak to a witness
**Before interviewing a witness, PEJ makes sure that:**
- A. The witness has a lawyer present
- B. **The witness's psychosocial care is attended to — that they are "fit for purpose"** ✓
- C. The interview is filmed
- D. Payment has been arranged
Correct: She stresses attending to the witness's psychosocial care and confirming they are fit for purpose *before* extracting information — a do-no-harm step.
Incorrect: Around 9:19 she describes checking the witness is "fit for purpose" before speaking.

## 5 · @ 10:00 — The thing field documenters miss
**Which US-criminal-practice concept does Cynthia say a well-meaning field documenter often does not follow?**
- A. Jury selection
- B. Plea bargaining
- C. **Chain of custody and the authenticity of evidence** ✓
- D. Cross-examination
Correct: Documenters take a statement, but without a strict chain-of-custody regime the evidence may be unusable in court — exactly the gap PEJ exists to close.
Incorrect: Listen at 8:47–9:40 — she uses chain of custody as her "really simple concept."

## 6 · @ 10:40 — The ICC's Miranda
**The ICC warning that mirrors Miranda rights — counsel, silence, and being informed of the accusation — is called:**
- A. An Article 15 submission
- B. **An Article 55 warning** ✓
- C. An Article 70 caution
- D. A Rome caution
Correct: She corrects herself to Article 55 of the Rome Statute, which sets out a suspect's rights during investigation.
Incorrect: She first says "52" then corrects to 55 — Article 55.

## 7 · @ 13:55 — How suspects are actually caught
**Because the ICC has no police force of its own, apprehending a suspect depends mainly on:**
- A. Interpol arrest teams
- B. **The cooperation of states (national authorities making the arrest)** ✓
- C. ICC marshals
- D. Military intervention
Correct: There is no international police force; Jean-Pierre Bemba was apprehended by the cooperative Belgian authorities in Brussels. Uncooperative hosts can shelter a suspect.
Incorrect: Re-watch 13:26–14:00 — the court relies on state cooperation, not its own police.

## 8 · @ 14:20 — Who pays for the court
**The ICC is funded and empowered by:**
- A. The United Nations General Assembly
- B. **The States Parties that ratified the Rome Statute** ✓
- C. NATO
- D. Interpol
Correct: The court is separate from the UN and funded by the States Parties — the countries that signed and ratified the Rome Statute. (As she notes, the US "doesn't pay the rent.")
Incorrect: Around 13:51 she stresses it is *not* controlled or funded by the UN — the States Parties fund it.

## 9 · @ 16:05 — The Bemba theory of the case
**In the Bemba case, Cynthia helped build the pre-trial theory around:**
- A. Financial fraud
- B. Drug trafficking
- C. **Rape as a weapon of war, under command responsibility** ✓
- D. Piracy
Correct: She centralised the topic of rape as a weapon of war and worked on command responsibility (his failure to punish). The trial conviction was later overturned on appeal on the weight of the evidence.
Incorrect: Listen at 14:38–15:52 — she names rape as a weapon of war and command responsibility.

## 10 · @ 20:00 — The sentence ceiling
**According to Cynthia, the ICC has no death penalty and a maximum sentence of:**
- A. Life without parole
- B. 18 years
- C. **30 years** ✓
- D. Death in the most serious cases
Correct: She states the court does not use the death penalty and caps sentences at 30 years despite the gravity of the crimes. (For accuracy: the Rome Statute also permits life imprisonment in exceptional cases — a nuance she leaves out.)
Incorrect: Around 19:32 she gives a maximum "cap ... of 30 years" and no death penalty.

## 11 · @ 21:00 — Why not Syria
**Why does the ICC lack jurisdiction over crimes committed in Syria?**
- A. Because Syria is too dangerous to enter
- B. **Because Syria is not a State Party — it never ratified the Rome Statute — so the only route is a UN Security Council referral** ✓
- C. Because no crimes occurred there
- D. Because the United States vetoed the case
Correct: Jurisdiction is largely dictated by the countries that ratified the Rome Statute; for a non-party like Syria the only door is a (politically fraught) UN Security Council referral — as happened for Sudan's al-Bashir and Libya's Qaddafi.
Incorrect: Re-watch 20:23–20:47 — the block is that Syria is not a State Party.

## 12 · @ 26:40 — What Project Expedite Justice fixes
**What core problem from her ICC years is PEJ built to solve?**
- A. Judges are too slow
- B. **Civil-society groups collect evidence in ways the court often cannot use; PEJ strengthens contemporary evidence collection so cases hold up** ✓
- C. There are too few prosecutors
- D. Trials cost too much
Correct: She reflected that field-collected evidence frequently "can't be used," so PEJ works to make the evidence — and the case — stronger.
Incorrect: Around 25:47–26:26 she describes making cases stronger through better contemporary evidence collection.

## 13 · @ 29:20 — Jurisdictional mapping and Article 15
**PEJ's "jurisdictional mapping" and an "Article 15 submission" mean, respectively:**
- A. **Which national, regional, or international forum could hear a crime on a given territory — and a request asking the ICC Prosecutor to open an investigation** ✓
- B. Drawing the crime scene, and a plea deal
- C. A witness list, and a subpoena
- D. A budget, and an appeal
Correct: Mapping asks which treaties a country signed and which courts are available; an Article 15 submission is a complaint requesting the ICC open an investigation.
Incorrect: Listen at 26:37–29:15 — mapping = available forums; Article 15 = request to open an investigation.

## 14 · @ 32:00 — When the national court is "futile"
**If pursuing a case in the national courts would be "futile" — say, systemic discrimination against all women — PEJ can:**
- A. Give up
- B. **Plead that the national avenue is futile and escalate to a regional mechanism or the ICC** ✓
- C. Bribe a local official
- D. Wait ten years and refile
Correct: Futility is the legal standard she names; establishing it lets you move to a regional body (AU courts, the human-rights court in the Gambia) or the international level.
Incorrect: Re-watch 31:04–31:41 — futility is the hurdle you plead to move up a level.

## 15 · @ 34:15 — The next wave: finance and human rights
**The "next set of waves" Cynthia describes pursues human-rights harm by:**
- A. Ignoring companies and focusing only on soldiers
- B. **Targeting corporate actors who fund or manufacture the weapons — naming responsible individuals and choosing a venue (OFAC sanctions, or the manufacturer's home court)** ✓
- C. Lobbying the UN to change the Rome Statute
- D. Publishing documentaries
Correct: She frames the finance–human-rights intersection: e.g. a bomb manufacturer with a New York bank account (OFAC), or an Italian manufacturer sued for corporate malfeasance in Italy — always naming responsible individuals and picking the venue like a "deck of cards."
Incorrect: Listen at 31:41–34:08 — follow the money: who funds and makes the weapons.
